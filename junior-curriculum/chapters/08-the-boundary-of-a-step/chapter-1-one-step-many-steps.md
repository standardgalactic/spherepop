# The Boundary of a Step

## Chapter 1: One Step, Many Steps

> **Circle reminder.** A circle holds a piece that belongs together, and circles can sit inside circles. To **pop** a ready circle, replace it with its value and leave everything outside it alone. In this book, a circle is *ready* when everything needed for its calculation is known and its operation is on the menu. To **redraw**, move circles around or split a number, as long as the value stays the same. The full rules are in the introduction.

Ibo and Sade were having a race. The question was **6 × 7**, and the winner would be whoever finished in the fewest steps.

Ibo said, "One step. It's 42."

Sade said, "Five steps. I did 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7."

Their cousin Tomas laughed. "I counted up from zero, one number at a time, until I hit 42. That's forty-two steps."

All three got the same answer. All three told the truth about how many steps they took. So who won?

### Step 1: Three ways to count

The trouble is that nobody had agreed what counts as *one step*.

**Ibo's way:** Ibo knows his times tables, so "6 × 7 = 42" is one fact, one step.

**Sade's way:** Sade added 7 to itself. Six sevens need five additions: 7 + 7 = 14, then 21, 28, 35, 42. Each addition is one step, so five steps.

**Tomas's way:** Tomas counted by ones. Counting from 0 to 42 is forty-two steps.

Same problem, same answer, and three different step counts: 1, 5 and 42. Nobody cheated. They just drew the boundary of a step in three different places.

### Step 2: Big steps hide small ones

Ibo's one step is really a bundle. Behind it sits Sade's five additions. Behind each addition, if you went even smaller, sits a bit of counting.

A calculator does this too. When you press the × button, the little chip inside carries out many tiny steps in a blink. You count it as one step, because you cannot see the tiny ones and you do not need to.

So "how many steps?" really means *"how many steps, if these are the kinds of step we allow?"*

### In circles

A **step** is one pop. The menu says which circles count as *ready*: a circle is ready when everything needed for its calculation is known and its operation is on the menu.

With times tables on the menu, Ibo's circle is ready at once:

```
( 6 × 7 )  →  42        1 pop
```

With only addition on the menu, ( 6 × 7 ) is not ready, so Sade redraws it first:

```
( 6 × 7 )
redraw  →  ( ( ( ( ( 7 + 7 ) + 7 ) + 7 ) + 7 ) + 7 )
pop ( 7 + 7 )   →  14
pop ( 14 + 7 )  →  21
pop ( 21 + 7 )  →  28
pop ( 28 + 7 )  →  35
pop ( 35 + 7 )  →  42   5 pops
```

Same circle, same value, a different number of pops. The menu decides how many.

### Two counts

Look at Sade's trace again. It has 5 pops and 1 redraw. There are two honest ways to count it.

- **Calculation steps** count only the pops: Sade has 5, and Ibo has 1.
- **Recorded moves** count every line of the trace, including redraws and refusals: Sade has 6, and Ibo has 1.

Neither count is wrong. They answer different questions. The first asks how much arithmetic was done, and the second asks how much the notebook had to record. A race needs one of them chosen and stated, and the choice can change who looks fastest.

### Step 3: A menu of steps

Imagine a game where only three kinds of step are allowed: **double**, **halve** and **add**. That is the whole menu. You may not just multiply.

Can you find **5 × 9** with that menu?

- Double 9 to get 18. (1 step)
- Double 18 to get 36. (2 steps)
- Now 36 is four 9s. You need five, so add one more 9: 36 + 9 = 45. (3 steps)

So 5 × 9 = 45 in three steps.

Now try **13 × 7**. Double 7 three times: 14, 28, 56. That is three steps, and 56 is eight 7s. You need thirteen 7s, and 13 is 8 + 4 + 1. So add 56 + 28 + 7:

56 + 28 = 84, then 84 + 7 = 91.

That is two more steps, so five steps in all. And 13 × 7 = 91.

With a different menu, the same problems would take a different number of steps. If multiplying is allowed, 13 × 7 is a single step. If only counting by ones is allowed, it takes ninety-one.

### Step 4: A fair race

Mina and Dev raced to find **99 × 17**.

Mina did: 100 × 17 = 1,700, then 1,700 − 17 = 1,683. She counted **2 steps**.

Dev did: 90 × 17 = 1,530, then 9 × 17 = 153, then 1,530 + 153 = 1,683. He counted **3 steps**.

Mina said she won. Dev was not so sure. "Is 100 × 17 really one step?" he asked. "I'd have to think about that one."

Here is the honest answer: *it depends on the menu.* If every multiplication counts as one step, and so does every addition and subtraction, then Mina wins, 2 steps against 3. If the menu is different, the count may change.

So before any race, any contest or any claim that one solution is shorter, the first question is: **what counts as a step?** Write the menu down. Then count.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What stayed the same for Ibo, Sade and Tomas?** (The answer, 42.)
- **What changed?** (How many steps they counted. 1, 5 and 42.)
- **What was missing from the race?** (An agreement about what counts as a step.)

### What makes this comparison trustworthy?

A comparison of step counts is trustworthy only if everyone used **the same menu**. Counting Ibo's one big step against Tomas's forty-two tiny ones is like comparing a mile with a kilometer by looking only at the numbers 1 and 1.6. Different units, different counts.

The answer to "which is shorter?" can be reliable and fair, but only after the menu is stated.

### Practice

1. Count the steps for **8 × 6** in three ways: as a times-table fact, as repeated addition of 6s, and by counting up from 0 by ones.

2. Using only **double**, **halve** and **add**, find **5 × 11**. How many steps?

3. Using the same menu, find **9 × 7**. (Hint: 9 is 8 + 1.)

4. Mina says: "I took 2 steps and you took 3, so I win." What should Dev ask her before agreeing?

5. How many steps does **12 × 12** take if you add twelve 12s one at a time? Why might nobody want to use that method?

6. With only addition on the menu, how many pops does ( 4 × 9 ) take? Write the redrawn circle, and say how many recorded moves the trace has.

### Answers

1. As a fact: **1** step. As repeated addition: eight 6s need seven additions, so **7** steps. By counting from 0 to 48: **48** steps.
2. Double 11 to get 22, double 22 to get 44, then add 11 to get 55 (44 is four 11s, and 5 = 4 + 1). That is **3 steps**. 5 × 11 = 55.
3. Double 7 three times: 14, 28, 56. That is three steps, and 56 is eight 7s. Add one more 7: 56 + 7 = 63. That is a fourth step, so **4 steps**. 9 × 7 = 63.
4. Dev should ask: "What counts as one step?" If a multiplication, an addition and a subtraction each count as one step, Mina's 2 beats Dev's 3. But they should agree on the menu before counting.
5. Twelve 12s need eleven additions, so **11 steps**. Nobody wants to use this method because the same answer is available in one step, if you know the fact.
6. Redraw it as ( ( ( 9 + 9 ) + 9 ) + 9 ). The pops give 18, then 27, then 36, so it takes **3 pops**. With the redraw included, the trace has **4 recorded moves**.
