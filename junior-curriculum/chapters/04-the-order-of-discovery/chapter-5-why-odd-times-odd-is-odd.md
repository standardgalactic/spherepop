# The Order of Discovery

## Chapter 5: Why Odd Times Odd Is Odd

An odd number is one that leaves 1 over when you share it in twos. Kofi multiplied some odd numbers together.

```
( 3 × 5 )    →  15
( 7 × 9 )    →  63
( 11 × 13 )  →  143
( 5 × 5 )    →  25
```

All four answers are odd. Kofi wrote a pattern in his notebook: *odd times odd is odd.*

He had a rule from chapter 2: before trusting a pattern, look for a reason. Is there one here?

### Step 1: A reason in one example

Take 7 × 9. Both are odd, which means each is an even number plus 1.

- 7 is 6 + 1.
- 9 is 8 + 1.

Kofi split both and multiplied the pieces.

```
( 7 × 9 )
redraw  →  ( ( 6 + 1 ) × ( 8 + 1 ) )
redraw  →  ( ( 6 × 8 ) + ( 6 × 1 ) + ( 1 × 8 ) + ( 1 × 1 ) )
pop all four  →  ( 48 + 6 + 8 + 1 )
pop  →  63
```

Look at the four pieces: 48, 6, 8 and 1.

- 48 is even.
- 6 is even.
- 8 is even.
- 1 is the only odd one.

Three even numbers added together give an even number. Then adding 1 makes it odd. So the total, 63, must be odd.

### Step 2: The reason works for every pair

Nothing in that argument depended on 7 and 9. For any two odd numbers:

- the first is an even number plus 1,
- the second is an even number plus 1,
- multiplying gives four pieces: even times even, even times 1, 1 times even, and 1 times 1.

The first three pieces are even, and the last is 1. So the answer is even plus 1, which is odd. It works for 3 × 5, for 11 × 13, and for numbers with a hundred digits.

This is a reason, not just examples. Kofi can mark the pattern **has a reason**.

### Step 3: Contrast with a neighbour

What about odd plus odd? Try 7 + 9.

```
( 7 + 9 )  →  16
```

Even. The reason: each odd number is an even number plus 1. The two leftover 1s make 2, which is even, so the whole sum is even.

What about even times odd? Try 6 × 7. That is six sevens. Six is even, so the sevens can be paired off with none left over.

```
( 6 × 7 )  →  42
```

Even. The reason: if either number is even, the product has pairs with nothing left over.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did the split keep?** (The value, 63, in four pieces.)
- **What did it show?** (Which pieces are even, and that only the 1 times 1 is odd.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

The reason is the split into four pieces. It covers every pair of odd numbers, so it is stronger than any list of examples.

The examples are still useful. They suggested the pattern in the first place, and they check the arithmetic. As in chapter 3, the messy notebook finds the idea and the tidy one gives the reason.

### Practice

1. Split 15 × 21 into four pieces, add them, and say whether the answer is odd or even.

2. Is 13 × 17 odd or even? Explain without multiplying.

3. Is 14 + 9 odd or even? Give a reason with the split idea.

4. Is 8 × 25 odd or even? Which rule decides it?

5. Kofi says: "Even plus even is even, because I tried 4 + 6, 8 + 10 and 2 + 12." What reason could he give instead?

### Answers

1. 15 = 14 + 1 and 21 = 20 + 1. The pieces are 14 × 20 = 280, 14 × 1 = 14, 1 × 20 = 20, and 1 × 1 = 1. Their sum is 280 + 14 + 20 + 1 = 315, which is **odd**.
2. **Odd.** Both numbers are odd, and odd times odd is odd by the four-piece reason.
3. 14 is even and 9 is 8 + 1. So the sum is even + even + 1, which is **odd**.
4. **Even.** 8 is even, so 8 × 25 is eight lots of 25, which pair up with none left over. (It equals 200.)
5. Each even number can be shared into pairs with none left over. Putting two such numbers together still leaves none over, so the sum is even.
