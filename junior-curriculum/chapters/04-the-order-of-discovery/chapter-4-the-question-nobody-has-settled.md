# The Order of Discovery

## Chapter 4: The Question Nobody Has Settled

Kofi found a game with two rules.

- If your number is **even**, cut it in half.
- If your number is **odd**, multiply it by 3 and add 1.

Then do the same again to the new number, and keep going. Kofi's job was to see where the numbers end up.

### Step 1: Try 6

```
6    even   →  ( 6 ÷ 2 )  →  3
3    odd    →  ( ( 3 × 3 ) + 1 )  →  10
10   even   →  5
5    odd    →  ( ( 5 × 3 ) + 1 )  →  16
16   even   →  8
8    even   →  4
4    even   →  2
2    even   →  1
```

Kofi reached **1** after 8 moves.

### Step 2: Try 7

```
7 → 22 → 11 → 34 → 17 → 52 → 26 → 13 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1
```

This took 16 moves. The numbers went up as high as 52 before coming down. But they reached 1.

### Step 3: A pattern, but is it a promise?

Kofi tried 9, 12, and 27. Every one ended at 1. He wrote in his notebook:

*Every positive whole number, used as a starting number, reaches 1.*

Is that a promise? He looked for a reason, as in chapter 2. He could not find one.

Here is what people know. Computers have tried this game on a huge range of starting numbers, far beyond anything one could do by hand, and every positive whole number reached 1. But **nobody has found a reason that works for every positive whole starting number**, and nobody has found one that fails. It is a famous open question.

So Kofi marked his notebook:

*Every positive whole number, used as a starting number, reaches 1.* **Only seen so far.**

This is different from the odd-number pattern in chapter 2, which had a reason, and different from the prime recipe, which was false. This one is still open.

### Step 4: Why bother, then?

An open question teaches two things.

First, a pattern can survive an enormous number of tests and still have no reason behind it. Surviving tests is a kind of evidence, but evidence is not a reason.

Second, being honest about what is unsettled is part of good mathematics. Kofi's notebook can say *I do not know yet* and still be a good notebook.

### What makes this trustworthy?

The individual trails are trustworthy, because each move can be checked: halving an even number and tripling an odd one plus 1 are plain pops. The claim "every positive whole number reaches 1" is not, and the right label is *only seen so far*.

A trustworthy notebook keeps these two things apart: what has been shown, and what has only been noticed.

### Practice

1. Follow 5 until it reaches 1. How many moves?

2. Follow 10. How many moves? Compare it with the trail for 5.

3. Follow 12 to 1. How many moves?

4. Follow 1 for three moves. What do you notice?

5. Kofi tried 20 numbers and all reached 1. Can he write "has a reason" in his notebook? What should he write?

### Answers

1. 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1, which is **5 moves**.
2. 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1, which is **6 moves**. It is the trail for 5 with one extra move at the start.
3. 12 → 6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1, which is **9 moves**.
4. 1 → 4 → 2 → 1. It goes around in a loop, so the game would never stop if it did not end at 1.
5. No. Twenty examples are evidence only. He should write **only seen so far**.
