# The Order of Discovery

## Chapter 6: One Case Is Enough

Kofi wrote down a claim in his notebook:

*Multiplying a number always makes it bigger.*

He tried some examples.

```
( 5 × 3 )  →  15     bigger than 5
( 5 × 2 )  →  10     bigger than 5
( 8 × 4 )  →  32     bigger than 8
```

Three examples, three times bigger. Is the claim true?

### Step 1: Look for a case that breaks it

Kofi's teacher said: "Try to break it. You only need one case."

Kofi thought about numbers he had not tried. What about multiplying by 1?

```
( 5 × 1 )  →  5     the same as 5, not bigger
```

And by a number smaller than 1?

```
( 5 × 0.5 )  →  2.5     smaller than 5
```

One case is enough. Half of 5 is 2.5, and 2.5 is smaller than 5. The claim is **false**. A case that breaks a claim is called a **counterexample**.

Kofi rewrote the claim so that it was true:

*Multiplying a number by something bigger than 1 makes it bigger.*

Now there is a reason: if you take more than one lot of a number, you have more than the number.

### Step 2: Counterexamples are strong

Compare what examples and counterexamples can do.

- A hundred examples that fit a claim do **not** prove it. There might be a case no one has tried.
- One counterexample **disproves** the claim, for certain.

This is a strange fact. A claim is easy to kill and hard to prove. That is why mathematicians love a good counterexample.

### Step 3: More claims to test

**Claim: "Squaring a number always makes it bigger."**

```
( 3 × 3 )  →  9     bigger
( 1 × 1 )  →  1     the same as 1
( 0.5 × 0.5 )  →  0.25     smaller than 0.5
```

False, with counterexamples.

**Claim: "Dividing a number always makes it smaller."**

```
( 6 ÷ 2 )  →  3     smaller
( 6 ÷ 0.5 )  →  12     bigger
```

False. Dividing by a number less than 1 makes it bigger.

**Claim: "Adding a number to another whole number makes it bigger."** Is it true? Try adding 0: 5 + 0 = 5, not bigger. Add 1 or more, and the claim holds. The reason is that you are adding something.

Kofi sorted his notebook into three piles.

| Pile | Meaning |
|---|---|
| has a reason | we can explain why it must be true |
| only seen so far | examples fit, no reason yet |
| refuted | a counterexample breaks it |

### Step 4: How to look for a counterexample

Kofi learned where counterexamples like to hide.

- At the **edges**: 0, 1, and the very smallest or largest numbers.
- At the **special numbers**: one that is its own opposite, a number less than 1.
- At cases where the **claim says "always"** or "every".

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did the examples keep?** (A suspicion that the claim was true.)
- **What did the counterexample show?** (That the claim was false, for certain.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

A counterexample is trustworthy because anyone can check it by doing the arithmetic. If 5 × 0.5 is 2.5, and 2.5 is less than 5, the claim is dead, whatever else is true.

A claim that survives all attempts to break it is still only "seen so far" until it has a reason.

### Practice

1. Claim: "All prime numbers are odd." Find a counterexample.

2. Claim: "The sum of two odd numbers is odd." Find a counterexample.

3. Claim: "Squaring a number makes it bigger than doubling it." Test n = 1, 2 and 3.

4. Fix the claim in problem 3 by saying which numbers it works for.

5. Kofi tried 30 numbers and the claim held. Does he have a proof?

### Answers

1. The number **2** is prime and even.
2. For example 3 + 5 = 8, which is even. (Any two odd numbers will do.)
3. n = 1: 1 against 2, so squaring is smaller (counterexample). n = 2: 4 and 4, the same. n = 3: 9 against 6, bigger. The claim fails at 1 and 2.
4. "Squaring a number is bigger than doubling it when the number is bigger than 2." For n = 3, 4, 5: 9 > 6, 16 > 8, 25 > 10.
5. No. Thirty examples are evidence only, and one counterexample somewhere could still break it.
