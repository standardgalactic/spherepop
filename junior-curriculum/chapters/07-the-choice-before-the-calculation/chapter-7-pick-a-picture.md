# The Choice Before the Calculation

## Chapter 7: Pick a Picture

Rina had 3 shirts and 4 hats. How many different outfits, with one shirt and one hat, can she make?

She could list them in a messy row. Instead she chose a picture.

### Step 1: A table

Rina drew a table. Rows are shirts, columns are hats, and each box is one outfit.

|  | Hat 1 | Hat 2 | Hat 3 | Hat 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shirt 1 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Shirt 2 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Shirt 3 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

Three rows of four boxes each.

```
( 3 × 4 )  →  12
```

There are **12** outfits. The picture made it plain why the answer is a product: 3 rows of 4.

### Step 2: When the picture shows a rule

Now Rina says: *the red shirt must never go with the green hat.* Shirt 1 is red and Hat 4 is green.

The table shows that exactly one box is not allowed. She crosses it out:

|  | Hat 1 | Hat 2 | Hat 3 | Hat 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shirt 1 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Shirt 2 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Shirt 3 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

```
( 12 − 1 )  →  11
```

There are **11** outfits that are allowed.

This is the idea from The Boundary of a Step: the table lists every outfit that is **possible**, and the rule says which are **permitted**. Counting the permitted ones means counting all and taking away the refused ones, as in Count the Other Side.

### Step 3: A bar for fractions

Rina has 24 stickers and gives away three quarters of them. How many is that?

She draws a bar of 24 and cuts it into four equal parts.

```
|  6  |  6  |  6  |  6  |
```

Each part is 24 ÷ 4 = 6. Three quarters is three parts.

```
( 3 × 6 )  →  18
```

She gives away **18** stickers and keeps 6.

Here the picture turned a fraction into something Rina could see and count.

### Step 4: Choosing which picture

Different questions suggest different pictures.

| Question | Good picture |
|---|---|
| How many pairs (one of this, one of that)? | a table |
| What part of a total? | a bar |
| How far apart? | a number line |
| How many in rows and columns? | a grid |

The picture does not change the answer. It changes how easy the answer is to see, and how easy it is to spot a rule.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did the table keep?** (Every outfit, one per box.)
- **What did it make easy?** (Seeing the product, and seeing the refused outfit.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

A picture is trustworthy when every item in the problem appears in it exactly once. In the table, each box is one outfit, and none is missing or counted twice. The check is to count another way: 4 hats for each of 3 shirts is also 12.

### Practice

1. Rina has 5 shirts and 3 hats. How many outfits? Draw the table in your head and say the product.

2. She cannot wear shirt 2 with hat 1 or hat 2. How many outfits are allowed?

3. She gives away two thirds of 30 stickers. Use a bar.

4. A grid has 6 rows and 7 columns. How many boxes? Which picture is it?

5. Why is a table better than a list for the outfit problem?

### Answers

1. ( 5 × 3 ) → **15** outfits.
2. 15 − 2 = **13**.
3. Cut 30 into three parts of 10. Two parts: ( 2 × 10 ) → **20** stickers.
4. ( 6 × 7 ) → **42** boxes. It is a grid.
5. A table shows each outfit once and shows why the total is a product, and a rule shows up as a crossed-out box.
