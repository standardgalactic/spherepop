# The Choice Before the Calculation

## Chapter 4: Start From the Extreme

Rina's piggy bank held 12 coins, all nickels and dimes. Together they were worth 85 cents. How many of each?

She could guess and check for ages. Instead she chose where to start.

### Step 1: Pretend everything is the smaller coin

"Suppose all 12 coins were nickels," said Rina.

```
( 12 × 5 )  →  60
```

That is only 60 cents, but the bank holds 85. She is 25 cents short.

### Step 2: Swap coins one at a time

If Rina swaps one nickel for a dime, the number of coins stays 12, and the total goes up by 5 cents (a dime is 10, a nickel is 5).

How many swaps close the gap of 25?

```
( 25 ÷ 5 )  →  5
```

Five swaps. So there are **5 dimes** and 12 − 5 = **7 nickels**.

### Step 3: Check it

```
( ( 7 × 5 ) + ( 5 × 10 ) )
pop  →  ( 35 + 50 )
pop  →  85
```

85 cents, and 7 + 5 = 12 coins. ✓

Rina did not guess. She picked a starting point that was easy to work out (all nickels), looked at how far off she was, and found what one swap did.

### Step 4: The same idea on another problem

A farmyard has 10 animals, all chickens and cows. Together they have 28 legs. How many of each?

Start with all chickens (2 legs each):

```
( 10 × 2 )  →  20
```

That is 8 legs short. Swapping a chicken for a cow adds 2 legs. So ( 8 ÷ 2 ) = 4 swaps. There are **4 cows** and **6 chickens**.

Check: ( ( 6 × 2 ) + ( 4 × 4 ) ) = 12 + 16 = 28. ✓

The choice was to start at the extreme, where every animal was the same kind, and then fix the gap.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did the extreme start keep?** (The number of coins or animals.)
- **What did it change?** (The total value or legs, which we fixed with swaps.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

The reason is that each swap does the same thing: it adds a fixed amount (5 cents, or 2 legs) and leaves the count alone. So the gap divided by the amount per swap is exactly the number of swaps.

The check, as always, is to take the answer back to the original: 7 nickels and 5 dimes really do make 12 coins and 85 cents.

### Practice

1. There are 9 coins, all nickels and dimes, worth 70 cents. How many of each? Start with all nickels.

2. There are 15 bicycles and tricycles in a shed, with 40 wheels in total. A bicycle has 2 wheels and a tricycle has 3. How many of each?

3. There are 6 coins, nickels and dimes, worth 45 cents. How many of each?

4. Rina starts with all dimes in the piggy-bank problem instead. How much too high is the total, and how much does one swap change it?

5. Why does the swap trick need all the coins to be one of two kinds?

### Answers

1. All nickels: 9 × 5 = 45. The gap is 25, and each swap adds 5, so 5 swaps. That is **5 dimes and 4 nickels**. Check: 50 + 20 = 70. ✓
2. All bicycles: 15 × 2 = 30. The gap is 10, and each swap adds 1, so 10 swaps. That is **10 tricycles and 5 bicycles**. Check: 30 + 10 = 40. ✓
3. All nickels: 30. The gap is 15, and each swap adds 5, so 3 swaps. That is **3 dimes and 3 nickels**. Check: 30 + 15 = 45. ✓
4. All dimes: 12 × 10 = 120, which is 35 too high. One swap from a dime to a nickel takes 5 away. 35 ÷ 5 = 7 swaps, so 7 nickels and 5 dimes. Same answer.
5. With only two kinds, each swap changes the total by the same amount. With three kinds, the swaps differ, and the gap cannot be closed by counting equal swaps.
