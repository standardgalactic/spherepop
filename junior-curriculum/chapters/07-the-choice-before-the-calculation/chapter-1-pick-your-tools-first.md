# The Choice Before the Calculation

## Chapter 1: Pick Your Tools First

> **Circle reminder.** A circle holds a piece that belongs together, and circles can sit inside circles. A circle is *ready* when everything needed for its calculation is known and its operation is allowed by the current rules. To **pop** a ready circle, replace it with its value and leave everything outside it alone. To **redraw**, move circles around or split a number, as long as the value stays the same. The full rules are in the introduction.

Rina had a toolbox, and it was not full of hammers. It was full of *moves* for numbers: splitting a number into smaller pieces, giving a mystery a name, and choosing a good starting point.

Her grandfather, who fixed furniture, liked to say: "The job gets easy or hard before you pick up the first tool. It depends which tool you pick."

Rina found out he was right about arithmetic too.

### Step 1: The same problem, two ways

Rina had to find **25 × 36**.

**Way 1:** Split the 36 into 30 and 6.

25 × 30 = 750. 25 × 6 = 150. 750 + 150 = **900**.

**Way 2:** Notice that 36 is 4 × 9, so 25 × 36 is 25 × 4 × 9. Now look at 25 × 4. It is 100, and 100 × 9 is **900**.

Both ways give 900. The answers match, because splitting a number into pieces never changes what it is. But Way 2 turned the hard part into a number Rina already knew, 25 × 4 = 100, so there was less to do.

Rina had not started calculating yet. The big decision, how to split 36, came *before* any arithmetic.

### In circles

Rina's choice is a choice about where to draw circles. Both of these are redraws that keep the value of ( 25 × 36 ):

```
( 25 × 36 )
redraw  →  ( 25 × ( 4 × 9 ) )
redraw  →  ( ( 25 × 4 ) × 9 )
pop ( 25 × 4 )  →  100
( 100 × 9 )
pop  →  900
```

```
( 25 × 36 )
redraw  →  ( 25 × ( 30 + 6 ) )
redraw  →  ( ( 25 × 30 ) + ( 25 × 6 ) )
pop ( 25 × 30 )  →  750
pop ( 25 × 6 )  →  150
( 750 + 150 )
pop  →  900
```

Both end at 900. The first draws a circle, ( 25 × 4 ), that Rina already knows how to pop. That is why it is the better choice. The answer is not different, but the circles are easier to pop.

### Step 2: Choose a good starting point

Next problem: **99 × 17**.

Rina did not want to multiply by 99. But 99 is just one less than 100, and 100 is a lovely number to multiply by. So she chose 100 as her **reference**.

100 × 17 = 1,700. That is one 17 too many, because 99 is one less than 100, so take one 17 away.

1,700 − 17 = **1,683**.

In circles, Rina's move looks like this:

```
( 99 × 17 )
redraw  →  ( ( 100 − 1 ) × 17 )
redraw  →  ( ( 100 × 17 ) − ( 1 × 17 ) )
pop ( 100 × 17 )  →  1,700
pop ( 1 × 17 )  →  17
( 1,700 − 17 )
pop  →  1,683
```

The circle ( 1 × 17 ) is the leftover that Rina has to put back.

Here is another one. Add **17 + 18 + 19 + 20 + 21**. Instead of adding them one at a time, Rina picked the middle number, 19, as her reference. Each number is a little more or a little less than 19:

−2, −1, 0, +1, +2

Those add to zero, because the pluses and minuses cancel. So all that is left is five 19s: 5 × 19 = **95**.

Check by adding the long way: 17 + 18 + 19 + 20 + 21 is 95. It matches.

### Step 3: Name the mystery

Some problems have a mystery number in the middle of them. Here is one.

*A shirt costs 5 more than a hat. Together they cost 25. How much is the hat?*

Rina gave the hat a name: **h**. Then the shirt is h + 5. Together:

h + (h + 5) = 25
2 × h + 5 = 25
2 × h = 20
h = 10

The hat costs 10, so the shirt costs 15. Check: 10 + 15 is 25.

Naming the mystery was a choice too. Rina could have guessed and tested numbers for a long time. Giving the hat a name turned the problem into something she could rearrange.

### Step 4: Choices can be good or bad, but not wrong

A choice does not change the answer. It only changes how much work you do.

For 99 × 17, Rina could have picked 90 as her reference instead of 100. Then she would work out 90 × 17 = 1,530 and 9 × 17 = 153, and add them: 1,530 + 153 = **1,683**.

Same answer. But she had two multiplications and an addition, instead of one multiplication and one subtraction. The reference 100 was a better choice for this problem.

There is one thing to be careful about. A choice stays safe only if you **put back everything you moved**. If Rina rounded 99 up to 100, got 1,700, and stopped, she would be 17 too high. That is a wrong answer, and the fix is the correction from A Useful Wrong Answer: name what you changed and take it back.

### Step 5: The note you leave

Rina's grandfather wrote a note on every repair: *"Replaced the leg, because the old one was cracked."* The note did not say how he *knew* to look at the leg. It said what he chose and why.

A good number notebook does the same. It writes down the choice:

*"I split 36 into 4 × 9, because 25 × 4 is 100."*

The note does not have to explain how Rina thought of it. Sometimes a good idea arrives as a hunch, which is what Kofi's messy notebook was for. But writing the choice down means somebody else can check it.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What stayed the same when Rina changed tools?** (The answer.)
- **What changed?** (How many steps there were, and which steps were easy.)
- **What would have been lost if Rina rounded 99 up to 100 and stopped?** (The right answer. She would be 17 too high.)

### What makes this choice trustworthy?

A choice is trustworthy when it is an **exact rewriting**: when the new form is the old one, just arranged differently. 36 really is 4 × 9. 99 really is 100 − 1. A hat plus 5 really is the shirt.

A choice stops being trustworthy when something is changed and not put back, such as rounding 99 to 100 and forgetting the leftover.

### Practice

1. Find **25 × 48** by splitting 48 so that you get a 25 × 4 inside it.

2. Find **98 × 12** by choosing 100 as your reference. What do you have to take back?

3. Find **15 + 16 + 17 + 18 + 19** by choosing the middle number as your reference.

4. A notebook costs 3 more than a pencil. Together they cost 11. Name the pencil p, and find both prices.

5. Find **125 × 16**. (Hint: what is 125 × 8?)

6. Find **45 × 22** in two different ways. Which way took fewer steps for you?

7. Write **25 × 28** with circles so that ( 25 × 4 ) is ready to pop. Then pop everything.

### Answers

1. 48 is 4 × 12, so 25 × 48 is 25 × 4 × 12 = 100 × 12 = **1,200**.
2. 100 × 12 = 1,200, but 98 is 2 less than 100, so you have two 12s too many. Take away 2 × 12 = 24. 1,200 − 24 = **1,176**.
3. The middle number is 17. The differences −2, −1, 0, +1, +2 add to zero, so the total is 5 × 17 = **85**.
4. The notebook is p + 3. So p + (p + 3) = 11, which gives 2 × p + 3 = 11, so 2 × p = 8 and p = 4. The pencil costs **4** and the notebook costs **7**.
5. 125 × 8 = 1,000, and 16 is 8 × 2, so 125 × 16 = 1,000 × 2 = **2,000**.
6. Any of these works, and all give **990**. One way: 45 × 20 + 45 × 2 = 900 + 90. Another: 22 × 50 − 22 × 5 = 1,100 − 110. Another: 45 = 5 × 9, so 5 × 9 × 22 = 9 × 110.
7. 28 is 4 × 7, so ( 25 × 28 ) redraws as ( ( 25 × 4 ) × 7 ). Pop ( 25 × 4 ) to get 100, and then ( 100 × 7 ) pops to **700**.
