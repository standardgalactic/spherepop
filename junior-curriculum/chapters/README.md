# Junior Math Books: Draft Chapters

Draft chapters (73 so far) for a set of nine short books on reasoning about calculation. Audience: ages 9 to 11. All files are plain Markdown.

`00-instructor-preface.md` is a short note for instructors on where the notation comes from. `99-afterword.md` gathers the habits the whole series builds. Start with `00-introduction.md`, a short note for readers and grown-ups. It also holds the **circle rules** (circles, pops, redraws, traces) that every chapter uses.

## The books

1. **A Useful Wrong Answer** (8 chapters): estimates whose errors can be named and corrected.
2. **The Work That Vanishes** (8 chapters): the missing-corner trick for products like 48 × 52, and running it backward for squares.
3. **Two Boxes, One Answer** (9 chapters): what can be told from outside; chapter 4 adds windows onto the trace; evidence versus reasons; chapters 2 and 3 discuss "slop" (work with no visible check, and pages that are hard to follow), judging the page rather than the maker.
4. **The Order of Discovery** (8 chapters): the messy notebook versus the tidy proof, then patterns with and without a reason.
5. **Reversing the Calculation** (8 chapters): what an answer throws away, and what clues bring back; which buttons can be undone.
6. **What Must Remain Unresolved** (8 chapters): working with an unknown x and leaving a faithful note; two unknowns and clues about their sum.
7. **The Choice Before the Calculation** (8 chapters): choosing a representation before doing arithmetic, and choosing a starting point.
8. **The Boundary of a Step** (8 chapters): what counts as one step; chapter 2 covers exponents; chapter 3 compares diaries at different levels of detail; chapter 4 separates moves that are possible from moves that are permitted.
9. **Solving by Changing the Question** (8 chapters): reformulations and what each keeps and loses; changes that add or lose answers.

## The running example

Each book ends with a chapter on the same case, **48 × 52**, seen from that book's side (the case file is in the introduction): the estimate that owes 4, the cross pieces that cancel, the answer on the fridge, three routes with one answer, winding 2,496 back, an open note, why split at fifty, the step you never take, and the price of changing the question.

## Shared format

Every chapter follows the same pattern: a short story, a few worked steps, circles, pops and redraws showing the same move (often in a dedicated "In circles" section), a "What did we keep?" box, a "What makes this trustworthy?" box, practice problems with answers, and a bridge to the other books.

## Questions for reviewers

- Is the reading level and pace right for ages 9 to 11?
- Does any worked example or answer look wrong? (Please check the arithmetic.)
- Do the "What did we keep?" and "What makes this trustworthy?" boxes feel useful, or repetitive?
- Which chapter would you want to read next, and which feels weakest?
- In Two Boxes, chapters 2 and 3: is the discussion of slop fair and clear for this age?
