# Solving by Changing the Question

## Chapter 1: Ask It Another Way

> **Circle reminder.** A circle holds a piece that belongs together, and circles can sit inside circles. A circle is *ready* when everything needed for its calculation is known and its operation is allowed by the current rules. To **pop** a ready circle, replace it with its value and leave everything outside it alone. To **redraw**, move circles around or split a number, as long as the value stays the same. The full rules are in the introduction.

Pia had a habit that annoyed her older brother. Whenever he asked her a hard question, she answered a *different* question, and somehow that gave the right answer.

"What's 1003 minus 997?" he asked, hoping to trip her up.

"How far is it from 997 to 1003?" said Pia. "Six."

She was right. She had not dodged the question. She had asked it another way.

### Step 1: Subtraction as distance

Subtracting 997 from 1003 looks awkward, with all that borrowing. But picture a number line.

997 is 3 below 1,000. 1,003 is 3 above 1,000. The distance from one to the other is 3 + 3 = **6**.

The question "what is 1003 − 997?" turned into "how far apart are they?" Both ask for the same number, and the second is easy.

Here is another one: **83 − 29**. Instead of subtracting, count *up* from 29 to 83.

- 29 to 30: 1
- 30 to 80: 50
- 80 to 83: 3

1 + 50 + 3 = **54**. Check by subtracting: 83 − 29 is 54.

### In circles

Pia's move is a redraw. She changes where the circles are, so that every circle is easy to pop.

```
( 1003 − 997 )
redraw  →  ( ( 1000 − 997 ) + ( 1003 − 1000 ) )
pop ( 1000 − 997 )  →  3
pop ( 1003 − 1000 )  →  3
( 3 + 3 )
pop  →  6
```

The redraw is allowed because it keeps the value: going from 997 up to 1,003 is the same as going up to 1,000 and then on to 1,003. Whenever you change the question, ask whether the redraw keeps the value, and what it forgets.

### Step 2: Multiplying as a rectangle

Now a product: **13 × 14**. Pia imagined a rectangle 13 wide and 14 tall, then cut each side into a 10 and a leftover:

- 13 is 10 + 3
- 14 is 10 + 4

The big rectangle breaks into four smaller ones:

- 10 × 10 = 100
- 10 × 4 = 40
- 3 × 10 = 30
- 3 × 4 = 12

100 + 40 + 30 + 12 = **182**.

The question "what is 13 × 14?" became "what is the area of this rectangle?", and the area was easy to add up from four easy pieces.

### Step 3: An equation as a hot-and-cold game

Here is an equation: **x + 7 = 12**.

Pia changed the question to a game of hot and cold. She made a **gap**: the left side minus the right side.

gap = (x + 7) − 12

The goal is to find the value of x that makes the gap **zero**. A gap of zero means the two sides are equal.

- Try x = 3. Gap: (3 + 7) − 12 = −2. Too low.
- Try x = 4. Gap: (4 + 7) − 12 = −1. Closer.
- Try x = 5. Gap: (5 + 7) − 12 = **0**. Found it!

In this puzzle, the sign of the gap tells you which way to go: a negative gap means the left side is too small, so try a bigger x. (That is true for x + 7 = 12. In other equations a bigger x can push the gap the other way, so always look.)

### Step 4: What did the new question keep, and what did it lose?

Changing the question is powerful, but there is a catch. A new question is only as good as what it *keeps*. Before Pia trusts a change, she asks two things:

1. **What does the new question keep?**
2. **What might it lose, or change?**

Here is what each of her moves kept and lost.

| Original | New version | Kept | Lost or changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 29 − 83 | Distance between 29 and 83 | How big the difference is (54) | Which one is bigger. The subtraction was −54, but the distance is 54. |
| 13 × 14 | A rectangle with its sides labelled | The total area, 182, and both sides | If you write down only the area, which sides made it. A 12 by 1 rectangle and a 3 by 4 rectangle have the same area. |
| x + 7 = 12 | Gap equals zero | The solution, x = 5 | Nothing, as long as the gap is exactly left side minus right side |
| 12 cookies shared among 5 kids, whole cookies only | 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 | The amount per kid | The whole-cookie rule. Nobody can get 2.4 cookies. |

The last row is the one to watch. The division gave a perfectly good number, but the number did not fit the original question. The real answer is that each kid gets **2** cookies, with **2** left over.

### The rule: take the answer home

Pia has a rule she never breaks: **always take the answer back to the original question and ask whether it fits.**

- Did 6 really equal 1003 − 997? Yes.
- Did x = 5 really make x + 7 equal 12? Yes.
- Did 2.4 cookies each really fit "whole cookies only"? No, so go back and fix it.

Changing the question is a way to find an answer. Checking against the original question is how you know your answer fits. (It does not tell you that no other answers exist. That needs its own reason.)

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did Pia keep when she changed 1003 − 997 into a distance?** (The answer, 6.)
- **What can a changed question lose?** (Details of the original, like direction, or a rule such as "whole cookies only.")
- **What does Pia always do at the end?** (Take the answer back to the original question and check that it fits.)

### What makes this change trustworthy?

A change of question is trustworthy when you can say **what it preserves** and **what it drops**, and when you have **checked the answer in the original question**.

That is a stronger standard than "it gave an answer." Anything can give an answer. What matters is that the answer still belongs to the question you started with.

### Practice

1. Find **1005 − 995** by asking how far apart the numbers are.

2. Find **72 − 38** by counting up from 38.

3. Find **12 × 15** by splitting it into four rectangles.

4. Solve **x + 9 = 21** using the gap. What do you get for the gap when x = 10? When x = 12?

5. Twelve cookies are shared among 5 kids, whole cookies only. What does the division 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 lose?

6. **29 − 83** is a negative number, but the distance from 29 to 83 is positive. What did the distance version lose?

7. Redraw **52 − 48** as two gaps through 50, and pop every circle.

### Answers

1. 995 is 5 below 1,000 and 1,005 is 5 above, so the distance is 5 + 5 = **10**.
2. 38 to 40 is 2, 40 to 70 is 30, 70 to 72 is 2. Total: **34**.
3. 12 is 10 + 2 and 15 is 10 + 5. The pieces are 10 × 10 = 100, 10 × 5 = 50, 2 × 10 = 20 and 2 × 5 = 10. Total: **180**.
4. The gap is (x + 9) − 21. At x = 10 the gap is −2, so x is too small. At x = 12 the gap is **0**, so **x = 12**.
5. It loses the whole-cookie rule. Each kid gets **2** cookies, and **2** are left over.
6. It lost the direction, meaning which number is bigger. The distance is the size of the difference without the sign.
7. ( ( 50 − 48 ) + ( 52 − 50 ) ) pops to ( 2 + 2 ), which pops to **4**.
