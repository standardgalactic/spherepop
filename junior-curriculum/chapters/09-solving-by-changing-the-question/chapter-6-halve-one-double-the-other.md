# Solving by Changing the Question

## Chapter 6: Halve One, Double the Other

Pia was asked: **16 × 25**. She did not like it. So she changed the question.

### Step 1: Halve one, double the other

Pia halved 16 and doubled 25.

```
( 16 × 25 )
redraw  →  ( 8 × 50 )
```

She did it again.

```
( 8 × 50 )
redraw  →  ( 4 × 100 )
pop  →  400
```

And 4 × 100 is easy. The answer is **400**. Check: 16 × 25 is 16 quarters of 100, which is 4 hundreds. ✓

Another: **35 × 12**.

```
( 35 × 12 )
redraw  →  ( 70 × 6 )
redraw  →  ( 140 × 3 )
pop  →  420
```

Check: 35 × 12 = 35 × 10 + 35 × 2 = 350 + 70 = 420. ✓

### Step 2: Why the product does not change

Think of 16 × 25 as 16 rows of 25 chairs. Join the rows in pairs. Two rows of 25 chairs make one row of 50 chairs, so the 16 rows become 8 double rows.

```
16 rows of 25  =  8 rows of 50
```

The chairs did not change. They were only arranged in longer, fewer rows. So halving one number and doubling the other keeps the total.

### Step 3: What to do with an odd number

Halving only works neatly when the number is even. **35 × 12** has an odd number, 35, so Pia halved the other one: she halved 12 and doubled 35. That keeps everything whole.

What about two odd numbers, like **15 × 17**? Neither can be halved. This trick does not help, and Pia uses another one from the earlier chapters.

### Step 4: What the change keeps and loses

- **Keeps:** the product. Every step can be undone by halving and doubling the other way, so no answers are added or lost, just as in chapter 2.
- **Loses:** the original numbers. 4 × 100 no longer mentions 16 or 25.
- **Costs:** you must be able to halve an even number and double the other cleanly.

The change is good when it ends in a friendly number like 10, 100 or 1,000. It is a poor choice when it makes the numbers harder.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What stayed the same?** (The product.)
- **What was lost?** (The original numbers.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

The reason is the chairs: fewer, longer rows with the same chairs. The change can be undone by doubling and halving back, and a change that can be undone keeps exactly the same answer.

The check is a second route, such as splitting one number, as Pia did with 35 × 12.
