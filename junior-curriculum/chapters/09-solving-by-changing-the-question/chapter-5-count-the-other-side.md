# Solving by Changing the Question

## Chapter 5: Count the Other Side

Pia was asked: *how many of the numbers from 1 to 20 are NOT multiples of 3?*

She could list the numbers that are not multiples and count them. But she changed the question.

### Step 1: Count what you don't want

Instead of counting the ones that are not multiples of 3, Pia counted the ones that are.

```
3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18
```

That is 6 numbers. There are 20 numbers in all, and every number is either a multiple of 3 or not. So:

```
( 20 − 6 )  →  14
```

There are **14** numbers that are not multiples of 3.

The question "how many are not multiples?" became "how many are multiples?", and then a subtraction from the total. The second question was easier because multiples of 3 are quick to list.

### Step 2: When it saves real work

**How many numbers from 1 to 50 do NOT end in 0 or 5?**

Listing the ones that do not end in 0 or 5 means writing out 40 numbers. Counting the ones that do is quicker:

```
5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50
```

That is 10 numbers. So ( 50 − 10 ) → **40**.

**Three coins are flipped. In how many of the possible results is there at least one head?**

The results are listed by writing H or T for each coin, and there are 8 in all. Listing results with at least one head is a long job. But there is only one result with no heads at all: T T T.

```
( 8 − 1 )  →  7
```

There are **7** results with at least one head.

### Step 3: What the change keeps, and when it fails

Pia asked her usual two questions.

- **What does the new question keep?** The answer. Every number is on one side or the other, so the two counts always add to the total.
- **What might it lose?** It only works if you know the total and if each item is on exactly one side. If some numbers were both multiples and not multiples, or neither, the subtraction would be wrong.

The change is good when "the other side" is **short** or **easy to recognise**. It is a poor change when the other side is just as long as the first.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did counting the other side keep?** (The final answer.)
- **What made it easier?** (The other side was shorter and easy to list.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

The reason is that everything is on one side or the other, and nothing is on both. So the two counts add to the total exactly. The check is to list a small case both ways: from 1 to 6, the non-multiples of 3 are 1, 2, 4, 5 (that is 4), and the multiples are 3 and 6 (that is 2), and 4 + 2 = 6. ✓

### Practice

1. How many numbers from 1 to 30 are NOT multiples of 5?

2. How many numbers from 1 to 40 are NOT multiples of 4?

3. Two coins are flipped. In how many of the 4 results is there at least one head?

4. How many numbers from 1 to 12 are NOT multiples of 2 or 3? (Hint: list the numbers that are multiples of 2 or of 3.)

5. Why would counting the other side be a bad idea for "how many numbers from 1 to 20 are multiples of 3"?

### Answers

1. Multiples of 5 up to 30: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, which is 6. ( 30 − 6 ) → **24**.
2. Multiples of 4 up to 40: 4, 8, ..., 40, which is 10. ( 40 − 10 ) → **30**.
3. The results are HH, HT, TH, TT. Only TT has no head. ( 4 − 1 ) → **3**.
4. Multiples of 2 or 3 up to 12: 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, which is 8. ( 12 − 8 ) → **4**. (They are 1, 5, 7 and 11.)
5. You would count the 14 non-multiples first and then subtract, which is longer than just listing the 6 multiples.

*Bridge to the rest of the series:* In chapter 4, scaling both numbers kept a quotient. Here flipping to the other side keeps the count, as long as every item is on exactly one side. In The Order of Discovery, counting a short list is often enough when a long list would be hopeless.
