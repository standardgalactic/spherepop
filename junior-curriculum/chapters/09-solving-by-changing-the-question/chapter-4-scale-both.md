# Solving by Changing the Question

## Chapter 4: Scale Both

Pia was given a hard division: **360 ÷ 15**. She did not want to do it. Her brother said, "You can change the question."

"By how much?"

"Divide both numbers by the same thing."

### Step 1: Shrink both numbers

Pia divided both 360 and 15 by 5.

```
( 360 ÷ 15 )
redraw  →  ( 72 ÷ 3 )
pop  →  24
```

And 72 ÷ 3 is much easier. Check in the original: 15 × 24 = 360. ✓

The answer to a division does not change when both numbers are scaled the same way. Think of it as sharing: 360 cookies among 15 children gives each the same amount as 72 cookies among 3 children, because everything is smaller by the same factor.

Another one: **1,200 ÷ 25**. Multiply both by 4:

```
( 1,200 ÷ 25 )
redraw  →  ( 4,800 ÷ 100 )
pop  →  48
```

Check: 25 × 48 = 1,200. ✓

### Step 2: What this does not do to subtraction

Subtraction behaves differently. Double both numbers in **100 − 40**:

```
( 100 − 40 )  →  60
( 200 − 80 )  →  120
```

The answer also doubled. So scaling both numbers keeps a **division** answer but doubles a **subtraction** answer. You would need to halve it again to get back.

What each move keeps depends on what the question asks.

### Step 3: A catch with leftovers

Now take a division with a leftover: **17 ÷ 5**. It is 3 with 2 left over.

Double both numbers:

```
( 34 ÷ 10 )  →  3 with 4 left over
```

The 3 stayed the same. The leftover did not: it went from 2 to 4.

Why? Because the leftover is measured in pieces of the same size as the divisor. Two out of 5 is the same share as four out of 10. The leftover doubled because the pieces were twice the size.

So the scaling trick keeps the quotient, but the leftover must be scaled back if you want the original.

Another: **33 ÷ 6** is 5 with 3 left over. Doubled, **66 ÷ 12** is 5 with 6 left over. Both leftovers are half of the divisor.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did scaling both numbers keep in a division?** (The quotient, the whole-number part.)
- **What did it change?** (The leftover, which scaled along with the divisor.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

The trust comes from knowing what the new question keeps and what it changes. Scaling both keeps the quotient for division, doubles the answer for subtraction, and changes the leftover. And, as always, the answer should be taken back to the original: if 72 ÷ 3 = 24, then 15 × 24 should be 360.

### Practice

1. Find 450 ÷ 15 by dividing both numbers by 5.

2. Find 2,400 ÷ 50 by dividing both numbers by 10.

3. Find 900 ÷ 36 by dividing both numbers by 9.

4. What are 17 ÷ 5 and 51 ÷ 15? Compare the quotients and the leftovers.

5. 100 − 40 = 60. Predict 300 − 120 without doing the subtraction, then check.

### Answers

1. ( 90 ÷ 3 ) pops to **30**. Check: 15 × 30 = 450. ✓
2. ( 240 ÷ 5 ) pops to **48**. Check: 50 × 48 = 2,400. ✓
3. ( 100 ÷ 4 ) pops to **25**. Check: 36 × 25 = 900. ✓
4. 17 ÷ 5 is 3 with 2 left over. 51 ÷ 15 is 3 with 6 left over. The quotients agree. The leftover tripled, as the divisor did.
5. Both numbers are three times as big, so the answer is **180**. Check: 300 − 120 = 180. ✓
