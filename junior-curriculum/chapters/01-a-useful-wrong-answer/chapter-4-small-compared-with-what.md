# A Useful Wrong Answer

## Chapter 4: Small Compared With What

Theo made two estimates on the same day, and both were wrong by exactly 8.

**Estimate 1.** Four crates of 98 apples each. Theo said "about 400." The exact answer is 392. He was off by 8.

**Estimate 2.** A small bag of apples that really held 12. Theo said "about 20." He was off by 8.

The errors were the same size. But Theo felt that the second one was much worse. Was he right?

### Step 1: Compare the error with the answer

An error of 8 means very little when the answer is 392. It means a great deal when the answer is only 12. So Theo asked, "How big is the error **compared with the answer**?"

```
8 out of 392   →   about 2 out of 100
8 out of 12    →   about 2 out of 3
```

Check the first one. Two out of every hundred of 392 is about 7.84, which is close to 8. ✓

The second one is more than half. Theo's estimate of 20 was not even close: it was nearly twice the real 12.

So the same error can be tiny or huge. The size of an error only means something when you say **what it is small compared with**.

### Step 2: Where does the answer come from?

Theo's error of 8 came from rounding 98 up to 100 in each crate. Here is the named error, as in chapter 2:

```
( 4 × 98 )
redraw  →  ( ( 4 × 100 ) − ( 4 × 2 ) )
pop  →  ( 400 − 8 )
pop  →  392
```

The error is **4 × 2 = 8**. For 4 crates it is small. If Theo had 400 crates, the same rounding would give an error of 400 × 2 = 800, but the answer would be 400 × 98 = 39,200. The error grew, but the *share* stayed the same:

```
8 out of 392       →  about 2 out of 100
800 out of 39,200  →  about 2 out of 100
```

Rounding 98 to 100 is always an error of about 2 in 100, however many crates there are.

### Step 3: What is good enough?

Whether an error is acceptable depends on the job.

- Ordering apples for a stall? An error of 2 in 100 is fine.
- Counting the exact change owed to a customer? Even an error of 1 is not.
- Deciding whether a truck holds enough? It depends on how close the answer is to the truck's limit, as in chapter 2.

There is no number that is "small enough" for every job. What matters is the job, and a named error lets you decide.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What stayed the same when the number of crates grew?** (The share of the error, about 2 in 100.)
- **What grew?** (The error itself, and the answer.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

The error is named by the redraw, so it is exact: 4 × 2. The comparison with the answer is a separate step, and it is an estimate itself. "About 2 in 100" is a rough measurement, and it needs its own care: always say what you compared with.

### Practice

1. Theo says "about 1,000" for 49 × 21, and the real answer is 1,029. The error is 29. About how many in 100 is that? (Is it closer to 3 or to 30?)

2. An estimate of 600 is off by 31 from the real answer of 569. Is the share closer to 5 in 100 or 50 in 100?

3. Two estimates are both off by 5. One is for an answer of 500 and one for an answer of 10. Which is the worse estimate and why?

4. Rounding 99 to 100 gives an error of 1 in each. For 7 crates of 99, what is the exact answer, the error, and about what share?

5. Dani says: "An error of 8 is small." What question should you ask Dani?

### Answers

1. 29 out of 1,029 is about 3 out of 100, so it is closer to **3**.
2. 31 out of 569 is about 5 out of 100, so it is closer to **5 in 100**.
3. The estimate for the answer of 10. Five out of 10 is half of the answer, while 5 out of 500 is 1 in 100.
4. ( ( 7 × 100 ) − ( 7 × 1 ) ) pops to 700 − 7 = **693**. The error is **7**, and 7 out of 693 is about **1 in 100**.
5. "Small compared with what?"
