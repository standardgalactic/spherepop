# A Useful Wrong Answer

## Chapter 8: The Estimate That Owes Four

Ravi was handed a card from the case file: **48 × 52**. "About 2,500," he said at once.

"How wrong is that?" asked his teacher.

Ravi had no idea. He had an estimate, but he did not have a number for how far off it was. In this chapter he finds one, and he finds it without doing the long multiplication.

### Step 1: Say what the estimate changed

Ravi's estimate came from changing the problem. He moved 48 up by 2 to make 50, and moved 52 down by 2 to make 50.

```
( 48 × 52 )
estimate ( 50 × 50 )  →  2,500
```

An estimate is a loan. He borrowed an easy problem, and the difference between the easy problem and the real one is what he **owes**. From The Work That Vanishes, Ravi knows what that difference is when two numbers are the same distance from the middle: a small square corner, 2 × 2.

So the estimate owes 4:

```
( 48 × 52 )  =  ( ( 50 × 50 ) − ( 2 × 2 ) )
```

Ravi can now say the estimate is **too high by 4**, before he has the exact answer. Paying the debt gives it:

```
pop ( 2 × 2 )  →  4
( 2,500 − 4 )
pop  →  2,496
```

The error is the estimate minus the exact answer: 2,500 − 2,496 = **4**. A positive error means the estimate was too high.

### Step 2: Compare three estimates

Ravi tried two other easy estimates for the same card.

| Estimate | Value | Error (estimate − exact) | Can you name it before finishing? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 × 52 | 2,600 | **104** (too high) | Yes: 48 went up 2, and 2 × 52 = 104 |
| 48 × 50 | 2,400 | **−96** (too low) | Yes: 52 went down 2, and 2 × 48 = 96 |
| 50 × 50 | 2,500 | **4** (too high) | Yes: the corner, 2 × 2 |

All three errors can be named. The 50 × 50 estimate is the best of the three because its error is both small and easy.

That is what makes an estimate useful. A wrong answer is useful when you can say how wrong it is, and which way.

### Step 3: Small compared with what?

Is an error of 4 small? In a number around 2,500, yes. It is less than 2 in every 1,000. If the answer were 12, an error of 4 would be huge. So Ravi always asks *small compared with what*.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did the estimate 50 × 50 keep?** (The rough size, and the knowledge of exactly what it changed.)
- **What did it lose?** (The exact answer, until the correction of 4 was paid.)

### What makes this trustworthy?

The estimate is trustworthy because its error is a calculated number, not a hope. Ravi can name the error from the way the numbers moved: 2 down and 2 up gives a corner of 2 × 2. He did not need the exact answer to do it. After paying the debt, he can check by a second route, such as 48 × 50 + 48 × 2 = 2,400 + 96, which is also 2,496.

### Practice

2. Estimate **47 × 53** with 50 × 50. What does it owe? What is the exact answer?

3. Estimate **46 × 54** with 50 × 50. What does it owe? What is the exact answer?

4. Estimate **38 × 42** with 40 × 40. What does it owe, and what is the exact answer?

5. Ravi says, "2,500 is exactly right for 48 × 52 because it is a round number." What do you say?

### Answers

1. The numbers are 1 from the middle, so it owes 1 × 1 = **1**. The exact answer is 2,500 − 1 = **2,499**.
2. The numbers are 3 from the middle, so it owes 3 × 3 = **9**. The exact answer is 2,500 − 9 = **2,491**.
3. The numbers are 4 from the middle, so it owes 4 × 4 = **16**. The exact answer is 2,500 − 16 = **2,484**.
4. The middle is 40 and the distance is 2, so it owes 2 × 2 = **4**. The exact answer is 1,600 − 4 = **1,596**.
5. A round number is easy, but it is not the same as exact. 2,500 is too high by 4, and a good estimate says so.
