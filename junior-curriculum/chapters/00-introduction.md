# Before You Begin

A note for readers, and for the grown-ups reading along.

Mathematics is not only about getting the answer. People who are good at it do four things, over and over: they **try** something, they **correct** it when it goes wrong, they **explain** why it works, and they **check** it.

All four count. A crossed-out guess is part of the work. So is a tidy explanation, and so is going back to see whether an answer really fits the question.

Each book in this set follows one idea about calculating. Every chapter has a short story, a few steps you can try on paper, and two questions that come back every time:

- **What did we keep?** When we changed the problem, what survived: the answer, the total, the rules?
- **What makes this trustworthy?** What is the reason, or the check, that lets us rely on the result?

You do not need to be fast. Use a pencil. Keep a messy notebook, cross things out, and try the practice problems before you read the answers. If your answer differs from the book's, that is a good moment to find out why.

## What "pop" means in these books

In these books, **popping means completing a calculation inside a circle and recording what happened.** This is a special arithmetic use of a broader idea: making a choice, keeping its history, and keeping track of what remains possible.

Three different moves will appear, and the notebook always keeps them apart, even when they lead to the same answer:

- a **pop** completes a calculation and records the result;
- a **redraw** rewrites the problem without changing its value;
- a **refusal** records a move that was possible but not allowed, and why.

## The circle rules

Every book draws its problems with circles, and every chapter uses the same few rules. In the text, a circle is written as a pair of round brackets.

1. **A circle holds a piece of the problem that belongs together.** Circles can sit inside other circles.
2. **A circle is ready when everything needed for its calculation is known and its operation is allowed by the current rules.** That means no mystery numbers and no unfinished smaller circles that the calculation needs.
3. **To pop a ready circle, replace it with its value.** Everything outside the circle stays exactly as it was.
4. **To redraw, move circles around or split a number.** A redraw is allowed only if the value stays the same.
5. **If no circle is ready, you are stuck.** That is allowed. What is left is your note to yourself.
6. **A trace is a notebook with one line for each pop, redraw or refusal, in the order they happened.** A pop forgets how a value was made. A trace remembers. Worked examples show the whole trace. In your own work, a short explanation that a reader can follow and check is enough, as long as you could write the full trace if someone asked.

For example, here is a trace for a small problem:

```
( ( 2 + 3 ) × 4 )
pop ( 2 + 3 )  →  5
( 5 × 4 )
pop  →  20
```

## Split, Balance, Correct

One strategy comes back through the series. **Split** a hard number into friendly pieces, **balance** the pieces into something easy such as a square, and **correct** for exactly what the balancing changed. The missing-corner trick is the clearest case: 48 × 52 splits around 50, balances into 50 × 50, and is corrected by taking away 2 × 2.

## Case file: 48 × 52

One problem comes back in every book, so that you can see it from many sides. It is **48 × 52**. Here is its trace:

```
( 48 × 52 )
redraw  →  ( ( 50 − 2 ) × ( 50 + 2 ) )          check: 50 − 2 is 48, and 50 + 2 is 52
redraw  →  ( ( 50 × 50 ) − ( 2 × 2 ) )
pop ( 50 × 50 )  →  2,500                        exact value of the square
pop ( 2 × 2 )  →  4
pop ( 2,500 − 4 )  →  2,496                      nothing owed
```

Look for the case file as you read. The estimate that owes 4, the pieces that cancel, the same answer by different routes, and the price of changing the question are all this one problem.

Notice that 2,500 means two different things here. It is the **exact value of the square** 50 × 50. It is also the **provisional answer** to the original problem, with a correction of 4 still owed. A tidy answer is only earned when the work underneath is finished, and the notebook says which of the two you are holding.

Most chapters show the same problem in two ways: first as a story, then in circles. The story explains why. The circles make each step small enough to check.

*For grown-ups:* popping in these books is an arithmetic teaching operation: completing a calculation inside a circle and recording the result. Pops, redraws and refusals are kept apart in the trace even when they lead to the same answer.
