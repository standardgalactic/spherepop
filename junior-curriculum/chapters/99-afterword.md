# Afterword: What to Carry Forward

A note for readers, and for the grown-ups reading along.

Across the nine books, the arithmetic changes: estimates, squares, boxes, patterns, undo buttons, unknown jars, choices of method, counts of steps, and changed questions. A few habits stay the same.

## Three questions

1. **What did we keep?** When a problem changed shape, what survived? The answer, the total, the rules, or only a rough idea?
2. **What makes this trustworthy?** Is there a reason that covers every case, a check that catches slips, or only examples so far?
3. **Was the move possible, and was it permitted?** A calculator may be able to do something that the problem does not allow, and a rule may allow something that cannot be done yet.

## Three moves

- A **pop** completes a calculation and records the result.
- A **redraw** rewrites the problem without changing its value.
- A **refusal** records a move that was not taken, and why.

The notebook keeps these apart, even when they lead to the same answer.

## One strategy

**Split** a hard number into friendly pieces, **balance** the pieces into something easy, and **correct** for exactly what the balancing changed. The missing corner, the average from a guess, and the strips near 100 are all this strategy.

## One problem, many sides

The case file, 48 × 52, was an estimate that owed 4, a set of pieces that cancel, three routes to one answer, a pair you can wind back with a clue, a note left open until the distance arrived, a choice of where to split, a step you can skip with a reason, and a question that was changed and then paid for. A good simplification always remembers what it left out. That is what the box "What did we keep?" is for.

## Three labels

When a claim appears in your notebook, give it one of these labels:

- **has a reason**
- **only seen so far**
- **refuted**

A claim can move from "only seen so far" to either of the others. It never moves to "has a reason" by adding more examples alone.

*For grown-ups:* these books use circles, pops, redraws and refusals as a small teaching language for arithmetic. One observation carries over directly: when every move keeps the value, the order in which ready circles are popped cannot change a finished answer, though it can change the cost, and some orders may never finish. Skipping a piece that is thrown away and reusing a value that is already known are the two cost-saving moves the series returns to. Notes on the origin of the notation are in `00-instructor-preface.md`.
