# Two Boxes, One Answer

## Chapter 2: The Box That Sounds Right

The boxes from the last chapter had slots and numbers. These ones are different. They are *answer boxes*. You type in a question, and a long, neat, confident explanation slides out.

Today you have two of them, Box P and Box Q, and one question:

**What is 48 × 52?**

### Step 1: Two answers

Here is what Box P wrote:

> 48 and 52 are both 2 away from 50, so picture a 50 by 50 square with a 2 by 2 corner missing.
> 50 × 50 = 2,500.
> The corner is 2 × 2 = 4.
> So 48 × 52 = 2,500 − 4 = **2,496**.

Here is what Box Q wrote:

> 48 and 52 are both 2 away from 50, so picture a 50 by 50 square with a 2 by 2 corner missing.
> 50 × 50 = 2,500.
> The corner is 2 × 2 = 4.
> So 48 × 52 = 2,500 − 4 = **2,486**.

Read them again. They are almost word for word the same. They are just as tidy, and just as sure of themselves. They differ in a single digit.

Which one is right?

You already know how to find out. Go line by line, the way Kofi's tidy proof worked. Every line is fine until the last one, and there Box Q went wrong: 2,500 minus 4 is 2,496, not 2,486.

Write each answer as a trace, with one line for each pop, and the difference jumps out:

```
( ( 50 × 50 ) − ( 2 × 2 ) )
pop ( 50 × 50 )  →  2,500
pop ( 2 × 2 )  →  4
( 2,500 − 4 )
pop  →  2,496     (Box P)
pop  →  2,486     (Box Q)   this pop is wrong
```

A trace lets you check each pop on its own, instead of trusting the whole answer at once.

Notice something uncomfortable. If you had only seen Box Q's answer, with no Box P to compare it to, nothing about it would have warned you. It was neat. It was confident. It was wrong.

### Step 2: What "slop" means

People have a word for a certain kind of stuff: **slop**. The word is used for things that *look finished* but show little sign that anyone looked after them properly. It might be an essay, a picture, a comic, a TV episode, a homework page or a set of math answers.

Care can go missing in two places. One is **the check**: nothing shows that anyone made sure it was true. The other is **the design**: nothing shows that anyone took care over what it displays and how loudly. This chapter is about the check. The next chapter is about the design.

Three things are worth knowing about the word.

First, **slop is not about who made it.** A box can make careful, correct work, and a person can make work that nobody checked.

Second, **you only see the page, not the maker's mind.** A wrong answer proves there is an error. It does not prove nobody checked, because a careful maker can still slip. So a good detective says what is missing from the page itself, such as "I can't see a reason for this last line," and leaves the story of how it was made open.

Third, **neatness is cheap.** Anyone, or any box, can make neat paragraphs very fast. Neat paragraphs are not evidence that anyone looked at whether they were true.

That brings us back to the detective's rule from chapter 1: matching on the outside does not prove two things are the same inside. Box P and Box Q look the same from the slot. One of them is right.

### Step 3: Four detective questions

When an answer comes out of a box, a good detective asks four things.

1. **Can I check it a different way?** Try 48 × 52 as 50 × 52 minus 2 × 52. That is 2,600 − 104, which is 2,496. Box P matches. Box Q does not.
2. **Does every line follow from the line before?** Read it slowly. The slip is usually in one line, not spread everywhere.
3. **What would I see if it were wrong?** For a product like this, one easy sign is the last digit: 8 × 2 ends in 6, so the answer must end in 6. 2,486 ends in 6 too, so this test lets Box Q slip past. That is a useful lesson: some checks are weak, and a good detective uses more than one.
4. **Who, or what, checked this before it got to me?** If you cannot tell, treat yourself as the first checker.

None of these questions needs you to be better at math than the box. They only need you to be willing to look.

### Step 4: Being fair to the boxes

It would be a mistake to say "never trust a box." A box that is often right is very useful, the same way a friend who is usually right about directions is useful.

But notice what makes you trust the friend. It is not how confidently they speak. It is that you have checked their directions before, and they were right.

Trust should grow with checks, not with polish.

That is the lesson of Mara's board, too. Her guess about 7/8 inch was useful, but she checked a far-off mark with a long tape before relying on it.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What can you see when an answer box hands you something?** (Only the words. You cannot see whether anything was checked.)
- **What did Box P and Box Q's matching look keep?** (The shape of a good answer.)
- **What did Box Q lose?** (The correct number.)

### What makes this answer trustworthy?

Chapter 1 had two kinds of statements: *evidence* and *reasons*. Now there is a third thing that is easy to confuse with them.

1. "Box P and Box Q both sound sure." This is **polish**. It tells you nothing about whether the answer is right.
2. "Box P matches my second method." This is **evidence**. It can still be overturned.
3. "Each line of Box P follows from the one before." This is a **reason**. It covers the whole answer.

(Answers: Polish is not evidence at all. Evidence is useful. A reason is best.)

### Practice

For each one, say whether the box is **right** or **wrong**. Then check it a second way.

1. Box says: *28 × 32 = 30 × 30 − 2 × 2 = 900 − 4 = 886.*

2. Box says: *7 × 13 = 10 × 10 − 3 × 3 = 100 − 9 = 91.*

3. Box says: *8 × 13 = 10 × 10 − 2 × 2 = 96.* (Remember the missing corner from The Work That Vanishes.)

4. Find two different ways to check that 15 × 25 = 375.

5. A friend copies a neat paragraph about volcanoes into a school report without reading it. A person made this, not a box. Is it still slop? Why or why not?

6. Write the answer to problem 1 as a trace, with one line for each pop. Which pop is wrong?

### Answers

1. **Wrong.** 900 − 4 is 896, not 886. Second check: 28 × 32 is 28 × 30 + 28 × 2 = 840 + 56 = 896.
2. **Right.** 7 and 13 are both 3 away from 10, and 100 − 9 is 91. Second check: 7 × 13 is 7 × 10 + 7 × 3 = 70 + 21 = 91.
3. **Wrong.** 8 and 13 are not the same distance from a whole-number middle, so the 2 by 2 corner does not apply. The real answer is 8 × 12 + 8 = 104.
4. One way is 20 × 20 − 5 × 5 = 400 − 25 = 375. Another is 15 × 25 = 15 × 100 ÷ 4 = 1,500 ÷ 4 = 375. A third is 15 × 20 + 15 × 5 = 300 + 75 = 375.
5. **It can be.** The word is not about who made it. If the friend really copied the paragraph without reading it, nothing in the report shows that anyone looked at whether it was true or whether it answered the question. A detective would ask the friend rather than assume.
6. ( ( 30 × 30 ) − ( 2 × 2 ) ): pop to 900, pop to 4, and then ( 900 − 4 ) should pop to 896. The last pop, which gave 886, is the wrong one.
