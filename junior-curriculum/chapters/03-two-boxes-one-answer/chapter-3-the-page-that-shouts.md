# Two Boxes, One Answer

## Chapter 3: The Page That Shouts

Last chapter, Box Q gave a neat, calm, confident answer that was wrong. This chapter turns that around. Here are two pages, and **both of them are right.**

Each page explains why 48 × 52 is 2,496.

**Page A** shows a big 50 by 50 square. A small 2 by 2 corner is shaded dark. Under the picture are three short lines: *50 × 50 = 2,500. The corner is 2 × 2 = 4. So 48 × 52 = 2,496.*

**Page B** has a title in six colors: *AMAZING MATH TRICK!!!* Five cartoon mascots are shouting from the edges. Twelve arrows point in different directions. A joke about pizza runs along the bottom. Somewhere in the background is the same square, with the 2 by 2 corner drawn small and in nearly the same color as the paper. The answer, 2,496, is there too, in the same size as everything else.

Every number on both pages is correct. The check from last chapter cannot tell them apart. Yet many readers would find Page B harder to use, and it is a good example of the other kind of slop.

### Step 1: Three kinds of care

A page can be right and still be hard to follow. Making it easy to follow takes care over three things.

**Salience** means the important thing stands out. When you look at the page, the first thing you notice is the main point.

**Simplicity** means every part earns its place. Nothing is there just to fill space or to look busy.

**Relevance** means every part is connected to what the page is about. A joke about pizza has nothing to do with a missing corner.

You can test each one.

- **The squint test (salience).** Squint at the page, or hold it at arm's length. What do you notice first? Is it the main point?
- **The cover test (simplicity).** Cover one part with your hand and ask whether the page is harder to use without it. If it makes no difference to the job the page has to do, that part may not be earning its place. But ask who the page is for: a reminder, a friendly character or a second explanation can help some readers even when it adds no new fact.
- **The "so what?" test (relevance).** Point at each part and ask: *what does this have to do with the point?* If there is no answer, the part does not belong.

Page A passes all three. The shaded corner is what you see first. Nothing could be covered without losing something. Every line is about the answer.

Page B does badly on all three, for this job. The title is the loudest thing. Most of the decoration could be covered with no loss to a reader who wants the trick. The pizza joke has no connection to the problem.

### In circles

Imagine each part of a page drawn as a circle. A circle should hold only pieces that belong together, which is relevance. The circle holding the main point should look bigger or bolder than the others, which is salience. A circle that holds nothing the reader needs can be left out, which is simplicity. On Page B, every circle is drawn at the same size and weight, so no circle looks like the main one.

### Step 2: When everything is loud

Imagine a classroom where every student shouts at the same time. Nobody can hear anyone. Loud only works when most things are quiet.

A page like Page B has the same problem. The colors, mascots and arrows are all at the same volume, so none of them can point at the important thing. The corner, which is the whole point of the trick, gets lost.

There is a word for a page like this: **gaudy**. To the reader, it is too much, too loud, and too much the same.

There is something else to watch for. Some pages work mostly by making you *react*, fast, with big faces, big colors and big claims. Others work by *telling* you something, and they can afford to be quieter, because the something does the work. When a page is all reaction and no point, you can ask what it was grabbing you for.

### Step 3: Why hurry can show

Taking care over salience, simplicity and relevance takes time. Somebody has to decide what the main point is. Somebody has to cut the extras. Somebody has to ask, for each piece, *does this belong?*

Those are choices, and choices are the first thing to go when something is made in a rush. A comic page finished to meet a deadline, a TV episode made quickly to fill a schedule, a worksheet put together in ten minutes: any of them can skip those choices, and skipping them can leave a page that looks busy instead of clear.

But the reverse does not follow. A busy page does not prove a hurried maker, and a spare page does not prove a careful one. You see the page, not the person. So name what the page does to you, for example "I could not find the main point," and leave the reasons open.

Two things are worth keeping straight.

First, **fast is not the crime.** A quick sketch with one clear point can beat a slow, cluttered painting. Speed is just how care tends to get squeezed out.

Second, **exaggeration is not the crime either.** A map that draws a river wider than it really is, so you can see it, is exaggerating on purpose, and every bit of the exaggeration points at something. A cartoon that stretches the one thing that matters can be careful work. Exaggeration with nothing to point at is just noise.

The honest test is never "who made it?" or "how fast?" It is the three questions: does the main point stand out, does every part earn its place, and does every part belong?

### Step 4: Two questions, four pages

Now we have two questions for any page that comes out of a box, a printer or a person. Is it **right**? Is it **clear for the job it has to do**? Each answer can be yes or no, so there are four kinds of page.

| | Clear for this task | Hard to follow |
|---|---|---|
| **Right** | Page A | Page B |
| **Wrong** | Box Q's neat answer | A loud page with a wrong answer |

You cannot tell which square a page belongs in just by glancing at it. A calm page can be wrong (Box Q). A loud page can be right (Page B). Both questions need a real look.

### What did we keep?

Write the answers in your notebook.

- **What did Page A and Page B keep in common?** (The correct answer, 2,496.)
- **What did Page B lose?** (Clarity. The reader's attention gets pulled away from the corner.)
- **Could the check from chapter 2 have warned us about Page B?** (No. Page B is right. Its problem is design.)

### What makes this page trustworthy?

Chapter 2 gave three kinds of statements: polish, evidence and reasons. This chapter adds a warning. **Being right does not make a page clear, and being clear does not make a page right.** A trustworthy page needs both, and each needs its own look.

### Practice

1. **Page C** has the square, the shaded corner and three plain lines of writing, just like Page A. But its last line says *48 × 52 = 2,486*. Is Page C's problem a design problem or a check problem? Which questions would catch it?

2. **Page D** explains the missing corner. It also has a paragraph on the history of squares, a joke about a dog, and a rainbow border. Use the cover test. Which parts would you remove? Is there any part you would keep only if it connected to the point?

3. Think of a comic page, a TV episode or an advert you have seen that felt too busy. Apply the squint test, the cover test and the "so what?" test. What did you find?

4. Fix Page B. Write down what you would delete, what you would make bigger, and what you would make quieter.

5. True or false, and why:
   - (a) "A page made by a person cannot be slop."
   - (b) "A page made quickly is always slop."

### Answers

1. It is a **check problem**. The design is clean, but the last line is wrong. The second-method check (50 × 52 − 2 × 52 = 2,496) would catch it.
2. The dog joke and the rainbow border fail the cover test, since the page is no worse without them. The history paragraph stays only if it helps explain the trick. If it wanders, it goes too.
3. Open answer. A good answer names one specific part that failed a test, for example "the background characters could be covered with no loss."
4. Delete the mascots, the arrows and the pizza joke. Make the 2 by 2 corner large and give it a color that stands out from the paper. Shrink the title and make it one color. Keep the three-line explanation.
5. (a) **False.** A person can make a page that is unchecked or hard to follow, just as a box can. (b) **False.** A quick sketch with a clear point is not slop. The word is about how a page serves its reader, not how fast it was made.
